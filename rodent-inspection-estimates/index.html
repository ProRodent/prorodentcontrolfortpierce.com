What gets looked at.

The technician works from the outside in. The tour begins at ground level with fences, slab edges, vents and utility entries, then climbs to the roofline and ends in the attic, the garage and any crawl space. Each stop answers a separate question about how an animal moves.

Evidence is read in layers. Pellets reveal size and freshness, rub marks reveal routes and chewing reveals how determined the animal is. Together they point to a species and a rough time frame.

Entry points in a humid climate.

Where air stays salty and damp, vent screens corrode, trim swells and sealant cracks sooner than in dry regions. Those tired parts become the gaps rodents use. Every one is recorded with a photo and a measurement, so the quote rests on facts.

What you leave with.

You get a plain account of the findings, a marked list of entries and a written plan. Each stage is its own priced line, so nothing is bundled in without a reason and you can approve only what you need.

  • Species and a likely head count.
  • Each entry point, listed by location.
  • A suggested order for the work.
  • A separate price for every stage.

Getting ready.

Clear the floor under the attic hatch and in front of the crawl space door, and move things off the garage wall. Move pets to another room and keep a flashlight handy if the attic is dark. Hold on to any photos, labeled with date and place.

Skip bait and glue boards before the visit. They shift where animals travel and can erase the signs the technician needs.

Next steps.

Most plans open with trapping and removal and move to entry point sealing. When the species is obvious, you may prefer the mouse page or the rat page.

Small jobs before we come.

  • A truck with debris under the hood stays parked until someone inspects it.
  • Carry grass seed, kibble and bird food indoors for now.
  • Drop any gnawed object in a bag and hold it for the technician.
  • Learn where the breaker panel is, in case a cable was chewed.

The CDC cleanup method in everyday words

Health agencies agree on most of the cleaning method, and the common ground is boiled down here.

  • Wet it first, wipe it second, and sweep nothing.
  • Leave the doors of a closed-up area wide open for half an hour before you start.
  • You cannot see germs, so glove up first and wash last.
  • Disinfect first and collect afterward, after the soaked material has rested about five minutes.
  • One securely tied bag can hold pellets, towels and a carcass.
  • Feeling feverish or short of breath after exposure is a question for a physician, so describe the cleanup to one.

Inspection questions asked in Fort Pierce.

How long will the visit last?

Many finish within an hour. A big building or a complicated roofline can take longer, and the technician explains the findings before leaving.

Will I get a price that day?

In most cases, yes. A quote comes after the walk-through because a number given over the phone is only a guess.

What if the technician finds no rodents?

You are told so directly, along with other possible sources of the noise such as plumbing, birds or other wildlife.

Do I have to be there?

Someone should be present to open doors and answer questions. For a rental, tell the property manager before the visit.

Can I book at night?

Call any hour and book. Visits usually take place in the working day, sooner if a slot opens.

What typically comes after an inspection.

  • Business sites.

    Return visits with paperwork fit landlords, managers and tenants sharing walls and bins.

  • Trapping.

    Snap traps or closed stations, chosen for the animal and the household, then rechecked on a schedule you hear first.

  • Sealing.

    The lasting half of the job: every opening located, measured and covered in chew-proof metal.
