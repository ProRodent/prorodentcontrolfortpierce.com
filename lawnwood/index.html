What matters for a Lawnwood call.

  • Yards and fences.

    Where lots have fences, sheds and plantings, animals often run along the foot of a fence or beneath a shed on the way to the house.

  • Garages and storage.

    A garage with stored boxes, seed or tools is a common starting point, and a worn door seal gives animals an easy test.

  • Roof and palms.

    Palm fronds and branches touching a roof can work as a bridge for roof rats, which are climbers.

Reading a residential lot.

A single-family lot has more edges than most people realize. The fence line, the base of the siding, the slab edge, the garage door and the roofline each give animals a chance to get in. We walk the outside first, then the attic and garage, following the evidence.

Palms, fruit and bird seed.

In a warm place where plants stay green most of the year, yards can offer rodents steady cover and food. Dense palm crowns, thick shrubs against the wall, fallen fruit and spilled bird seed all help. None of it needs to be dramatic: a small spill each day is enough to sustain animals.

  • Trim fronds and branches back from the roof and siding.
  • Put bird seed and pet food in lidded tubs, and carry bowls indoors after dark.
  • Pick up fallen fruit and cover compost.
  • Raise firewood and lumber onto a rack set apart from the walls.

The yard page goes through those changes in more detail, and the rat page explains how roof rats are handled.

Storm season and the walls.

Heavy rain and strong wind can loosen vent covers, bring down fronds and flood burrows. Animals may move toward a building to find dry cover. When a storm passes, circle the foundation and watch for fresh holes, flapping screens and debris piled on the siding.

If you see something new, photograph it and phone. A quick description often tells us whether it is a rat, a squirrel or something else.

Garages are a frequent starting point, as the garage page explains. If a truck, boat trailer or cart stays parked for weeks, look under its hood and into the cabin vents before the next start. The vehicle page has the checklist.

What to mention when you call.

Give your street, whether the lot is fenced, whether palms or trees touch the roof and whether you hear noise in the attic or walls. Add what you found and when it began. That lets the technician plan the visit before arriving.

Getting the property ready.

  • Pellets, nests and gnawed cords are off limits to small children and pets.
  • Prop open a shut-up shed or garage and give it time to air before anyone goes in.
  • Traps you already set can stay exactly where they are.
  • Mention any storm cleanup, roof work, palm trimming or shifted storage from the past month.

The CDC cleanup method in everyday words

Read each line before you touch anything, because a short pause protects your airways.

  • Mist anything dry before a hand, glove or towel comes near it.
  • A locked outbuilding is not for walking into at once. Let air move through it for about half an hour.
  • Disposable or reusable gloves both work, provided the gloves and then your hands get washed.
  • Timing matters: wet the waste, let it rest about five minutes and only then wipe, so dust stays down.
  • Tie the bag firmly before carrying it out, and wear a hand barrier whenever a carcass has to move.
  • Notice how you feel in the weeks that follow. A fever or shortness of breath is a reason to see a doctor and describe what you handled.

Do palms attract roof rats?

Dense fronds can offer shelter and a route to the roof. Trimming and removing dead fronds helps, but sealing and trapping are still needed.

Can you inspect a shed?
