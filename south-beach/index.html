What matters for a South Beach call.

  • Salt air and wear.

    Salt and moisture corrode screens, vent covers and metal trim faster than in drier places, which can quietly open gaps.

  • Seasonal gear and storage.

    Cushions, covers, beach gear and boat supplies make good nesting material when they sit untouched.

  • Vehicles that sit.

    A car, cart or trailer left for weeks can host a nest, particularly if it is parked under palms or beside stored items.

Salt air, sand and building wear.

Coastal air is hard on buildings. Salt corrodes light metal screens, humidity swells wood trim and sun cracks sealant. Each of those can open a gap the width of a pencil, which is enough for a mouse. A quick look every few months catches small gaps before animals do.

Seasonal homes and rentals.

A building that stands empty for weeks gives rodents time to explore. Closed-up rooms hold stale air, food left behind may attract them and nobody hears the noise. If you own a seasonal home or manage a rental, check it when you arrive: look for droppings, chewed items and nests, and air the space out before cleaning.

  • Open the building and let it air for thirty minutes before going in.
  • Check the pantry, closets and garage for droppings or gnawing.
  • Store linens, cushions and gear in lidded plastic bins.
  • Leave no food behind between stays.

The garage page and the exclusion page go through the checks in more detail.

Cars, carts, boats and trailers.

A vehicle that sits at a beach property, whether a car, a golf cart or a boat trailer, is a tempting nest. Warm engine parts and soft materials draw animals in. Look under the hood and in the cabin vents before starting any vehicle that has been left, and see the vehicle page.

Storms and the calendar.

Heavy rain and strong winds can loosen screens, drop fronds and flood burrows. After a storm, walk the property and check vents, seals and the base of the walls. Photograph anything new and phone if you spot droppings or fresh holes.

What to mention when you call.

Open with the street or nearest cross street, then say if the building is a full-time home, a rental or a seasonal place, and what you saw. Mention parked vehicles or boats. That helps us plan the visit and bring the right materials.

Getting the property ready.

  • Stay out of the attic and the space under the floor, since moving insulation throws grit into the air.
  • Leave a nest alone, because a startled mother may retreat deeper into the structure.
  • Work out where the attic hatch and the crawl door are before anyone arrives.
  • If a golf cart or truck shows nest debris, park it outdoors until it is checked.

Guard your lungs before touching anything

Come back to this routine each time pellets, urine stains or shredded nesting turn up indoors.

  • Anything dry stays off the brush and out of the vacuum.
  • Leave the doors of a closed-up area wide open for half an hour before you start.
  • Disposable or reusable gloves both work, provided the gloves and then your hands get washed.
  • Keep spraying until no dry patch remains, then step away for about five minutes before wiping.
  • Tie the bag firmly before carrying it out, and wear a hand barrier whenever a carcass has to move.
  • Feeling feverish or short of breath after exposure is a question for a physician, so describe the cleanup to one.
