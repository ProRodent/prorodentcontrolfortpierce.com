How a Fort Pierce property gets sized up.

Fort Pierce is the seat of St. Lucie County, a council-manager city on the Atlantic side of Florida's Treasure Coast. Salt air and warm, wet weather define the place far more than any boundary line, and rodents respond to weather.

Where it stays humid, rats and mice never really slow down. Signs can appear in any month, and storms or long rainy stretches can drive animals out of soggy cover and into drier shelter like an attic, a soffit or a stack of garage totes. A short evening walk outside with a flashlight tells you which of those conditions describe your lot.

Mouse, rat or something else.

A house mouse can slip through a crack hardly wider than a pencil, and a rat needs close to half an inch. Roof rats climb wires, fences and palm trunks, while Norway rats like to dig beside slabs, sheds and foundations.

Species decides the trap, the sealing metal and how cleanup is done, so the animal is named before any plan is written. What you describe by phone gives a head start, and the visit fills the gaps.

Storm season and the calendar.

Summer downpours and the odd tropical system can flood burrows and scatter debris, and animals often look for new shelter afterward. Once a storm passes, walk the foundation and note new holes, loose vent covers and any frond or limb resting on the roof.

  • Ahead of the rainy months, test vent covers and pipe collars, and check the low corners of the garage door.
  • Store kibble, bird seed and grass seed in tubs that seal.
  • Raise firewood and lumber onto a rack, clear of the walls.
  • Cut palm fronds and limbs away from the roof and the siding.

Until the technician gets there.

A few habits protect both the household and the evidence. Do not sweep up droppings, steer kids and pets away from that room and leave any trap you already set where it sits.

  • Photograph pellets, chew marks and damage, with a coin beside each for scale.
  • Jot down the room and the hour of each noise.
  • Close the door to the busiest room.
  • Keep loose bait and glue boards out of the picture, since both carry their own hazards.

Course of a visit.

A lone mouse in a pantry or a nest inside a truck engine: both jobs move through these four stages.

  1. Inspect.

    The technician begins wherever the signs are strongest, whether attic, garage, crawl space or yard, and traces the roofline, hunting fresh evidence and the openings animals use.

  2. Remove.

    Devices go on the routes the evidence reveals, picked for the species and tucked away from children and pets.

  3. Seal.

    Cracks, vents and chewed holes get flashing, mesh and a sealant that teeth cannot get through.

  4. Verify.

    A return trip checks the traps, tests the seals and takes care of any cleanup or repair still waiting.

Signs that rodents are in the building.

Find two or more of these in one corner, and the animals are probably active this week, not a leftover from long ago.

  • Droppings.

    Small dark pellets in a cabinet, along a wall, on a garage shelf or scattered across the attic floor.

  • New chewing.

    Recent teeth marks look light-colored and rough, on a wooden beam, a PVC pipe, a cable jacket or a cereal box.

  • Sounds after dark.

    Scratching, skittering or a muffled bump in a wall or over a ceiling once everyone has gone to bed.

  • Bedding.

    A wad of torn paper, tugged insulation, cloth bits or dry palm fiber jammed into a protected corner.

  • Rub marks.

    Dark, oily streaks on walls, pipes and joists show a route used night after night.

  • A bad smell.

    A sharp ammonia odor in a shut room, or a heavier rotten smell where an animal died.

Seen a few of these? Call before the chewing spreads.

A landmark helps us picture where a call is coming from, and it cannot tell us what lives in your walls. A quiet residential block, a lot beside open ground and a restaurant next to a loading area would each start us in a different place.

Questions from Fort Pierce households.

Is it a mouse or a rat?

Study the droppings. Tiny ones in the kitchen suggest mice, while chunkier ones near the roofline or in the attic suggest rats. A burrow next to a slab or a shed is another clue pointing to rats.

How do rodents get inside?

A mouse can pass a slit no wider than a pencil, and a rat needs about twice that. Usual entries are pipe and cable crossings, vent covers, garage door corners and the roof edge.

Will trapping fix it by itself?

Traps handle the animals already inside, while sealing blocks whatever comes next. Nearly every job uses both, trapping first, with a revisit to confirm.

Is the work safe around kids and pets?

Tell us who lives in the home ahead of time. Traps and stations are set out of reach, and you receive a note afterward listing each device and its spot.
