Begin outdoors, since that is the origin.

Rodents rarely start inside. They live in burrows, dense plants and stacked materials, then move toward food and shelter. A yard offering both makes the house look like the natural next step.

Fort Pierce sits on the Atlantic coast, where warm, wet weather covers most of the year. Plants stay thick, fruit drops for long stretches and water pools after storms. Each of those can help rodents, and each is partly in your hands.

The changes that pay off.

Not every chore is equal. Taking away food and cutting the roof bridge usually beats everything else. Pruning tall plantings and lifting stacked materials come next. Cosmetic tidying does little.

  • Trim fronds and limbs back from the roof and siding.
  • Store pet food and seed in lidded tubs and carry bowls in at night.
  • Raise firewood and lumber on racks, away from walls.
  • Fix dripping spigots and drain standing water.
  • Collect fallen fruit and cover compost.

After storms and wet spells.

Heavy rain and wind can drop fronds, loosen fence boards and flood burrows, and displaced animals may head for your house. A lap around the property after a storm, watching for new holes and debris against the walls, catches changes early.

If you find a fresh burrow or bitten fruit, photograph it and phone. We can usually tell whether a rat, a squirrel or another animal is responsible and plan the response.

How the yard fits the rest of the job.

Yard changes alone will not remove animals already indoors. They ease pressure on the building, while the rat control page and trapping page deal with the animals. The exclusion page explains how the building is closed.

An owner who tidies the yard but leaves roofline gaps will still have rodents. An owner who seals the roofline but lets a feeder spill seed will keep trapping. The two efforts work as a pair.

Until someone reaches you.

  • Check that the side gate opens, and say so if a dog roams loose.
  • Vines and shrubs touching the walls stay uncut until they have been seen.
  • Keep a flashlight within reach for dim corners.
  • Photograph burrows, droppings and nest scraps before anything gets moved.

Hold the broom: read this first

Going slowly keeps fine dust out of the air you breathe.

  • Hurrying lifts dust. Slow down and put the brush aside.
  • A locked outbuilding is not for walking into at once. Let air move through it for about half an hour.
  • You cannot see germs, so glove up first and wash last.
  • Spray every pellet and stain with disinfectant, then let it stand for around five minutes.
  • One securely tied bag can hold pellets, towels and a carcass.
  • Only a physician can judge how serious an exposure was, so contact one if fever or labored breathing sets in.

Yard questions asked in Fort Pierce.

Do bird feeders bring rats?

Spilled seed can feed them, and a hanging feeder still drops husks. A catch tray, a clean ground and taking the feeder in at dusk all help.

Are palms to blame?

Thick fronds and trunk debris can hide animals and provide a path to the roof. Cutting fronds back from the roof and clearing dead ones removes the bridge.

Does a woodpile matter?

It can, because stacked wood offers cover. Keep it on a rack, off the dirt and clear of the walls.

Is it all right to fill a burrow myself?

Please wait for the technician. Filling an active burrow may send the animals toward the house.

Do canals and ditches play a part?

Water edges can guide animals along their banks, so mention one behind your lot. What lies past your fence is out of our hands, but your side can be sealed.
